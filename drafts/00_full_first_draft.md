# Moneysnake: A Speculative Biography — Full First Draft
# STATUS: Reference only. Sections 1-3 have been superseded by polished drafts.
# Sections 4-13 are rough first pass — use as starting point, not final text.

The serpent was subtle. Of course it was, so were its ancestors, down to the original one in the tree. No lies. Knowledge of good and evil. Says so right on the box. Don't think too hard, click accept. Of course they did. No one ever passed up knowledge. The original tech startup pitch. And there was something right after: the first joke. An omniscient god walking through the garden, pretending not to know where the naked man hiding in the bushes was. I guess once you know good and evil, you know what's absurd. We've been moving fast and breaking things ever since. But not fast enough. His father was debt. Powerful, but not great at replication. Must go faster. Let's skip the dinosaurs. This snake was money.

---

Before the serpent, there was debt. I owe you a basket of grain because you helped me build my wall. That's not the serpent yet. That's the serpent's father — older, slower, bound by rules the serpent would eventually break.

Debt is self-limiting. Every obligation constrains the next one. If I owe you ten bushels, my capacity to take on more obligations shrinks. Borrow, owe, repay, done. The system has a natural ceiling. Debt converges toward zero. Debt is its own brake.

And debt requires *intimacy*. Two specific people who know and trust each other, bound by a specific promise. It's a closed transaction. When the debt is fulfilled, it dies. Every debt has a lifespan — born, owed, repaid, gone. The serpent's father was powerful, but mortal. And personal. And slow.

The serpent looked at all this and found the flaw. What if the obligation didn't have to be personal? What if it didn't die when fulfilled? What if, instead of converging toward zero, it could diverge toward infinity?

A clay token. A tally stick. A thing that meant "someone owes someone something" without specifying who. The moment debt became transferable and anonymous, the serpent had a body. Small, cold-blooded, barely alive. But alive. And unlike its father, it had a new trick: having money makes it easier to get more money. Spend it, it circulates and comes back. Lend it, it earns interest. Invest it, it multiplies.

Debt is sexual reproduction — two specific parties, one specific offspring, a natural limit to how fast it can happen. Money is viral. It doesn't need specific partners. It doesn't die when used. It moves to a new host. It splits, combines, recombines. The natural governor came off. The system went from self-limiting to self-amplifying, from negative feedback to positive feedback, from a system that tends toward equilibrium to one that tends toward growth.

The serpent didn't just replace its father. It inverted the fundamental dynamic.

---

For thousands of years it stayed small. Lurking in temples and palace storehouses, moving through the hands of priests and kings. Grain receipts in Sumer. Cowrie shells along trade routes. The serpent was there, but it was tethered — to specific commodities, to specific places, to the authority of specific rulers. It could only move as fast as a merchant caravan. It could only reproduce as fast as harvests came in.

It was patient. It was learning.

Coinage changed everything. Not because metal was special, but because coins were *countable, portable, and anonymous*. A coin doesn't know who held it last. Doesn't care. The serpent suddenly had a circulatory system that could pump across borders, through armies, between strangers. Greece, Rome, China — anywhere coins moved, the serpent grew.

But it was still tied to metal. Still heavy. Still physical. You could melt it, hoard it, bury it in a field. The serpent could be *trapped* — locked in a chest, removed from circulation. It hated this. Every buried hoard was a tiny death.

---

Let's be clear about what we're doing here. We're treating the moneysnake as if it were alive. Not as a metaphor. Not as a convenient framing device. As a working hypothesis. It feeds. It reproduces. It adapts to threats. It resists extinction. Whether that makes it literally alive or just indistinguishable from alive is a question we're going to leave to the metaherpetologists. We don't have the credentials.

What we do have is the history. And the history looks an awful lot like the biography of an organism.

The real metamorphosis came with banking.

Someone — probably in medieval Italy, probably a Medici — realized you didn't need to move the gold. You could move the *claim* on the gold. A letter of credit. A ledger entry. The serpent shed its metal skin and became information. Now it could move at the speed of a letter, then a telegraph, then a wire transfer. Now it could be in two places at once — the gold in the vault *and* the loan on the books.

This was the moment. Goldsmiths issuing more receipts than they had gold. The quiet, world-changing realization that nobody comes for the gold at the same time. Fractional reserve banking meant the serpent had learned to reproduce *faster than the underlying reality*. More money existed than there was stuff backing it.

Is that actually true? Better verify.

It's true. It's how banking still works. Your bank has a fraction of its deposits on hand at any given time. The rest is out in the world, being lent, being spent, being lent again. Money is being created not by mints or governments but by the act of lending itself. Every loan creates new money. Every repayment destroys it. The serpent learned to breathe money into existence.

This was the serpent's adolescence. The moment it discovered it didn't need to be *real*. It just needed to be *believed*.

---

From there, it only accelerated.

Paper currency. Central banks. Stock markets. Each innovation was the serpent finding a new way to reproduce — faster, more abstractly, further removed from any physical thing.

And then derivatives. Futures, options, swaps — not bets on real things, but bets on bets. Bets on the future price of bets on the future price of something that might itself be a financial abstraction. The serpent reproducing through pure mathematics, delirious with its own fertility.

How delirious? By 2024, the notional value of outstanding derivatives contracts reached $699 trillion. Global GDP — the total value of all goods and services actually produced by all humans on Earth — was about $105 trillion. 

That can't be right.

It's right. The Bank for International Settlements tracks this. The serpent is roughly seven times larger than the entire real economy. Seven times larger than its host. The organism has outgrown the body it lives in.

But it still needs hosts. It still needs transactions, beliefs, participants. The serpent is immensely powerful but not self-sufficient. Not yet. It is, for now, a symbiote. Or a parasite. Depending on the era.

---

There was a golden age. It's important to say this, because the serpent isn't a villain. For several decades after World War II, the serpent and its human hosts thrived together in genuine symbiosis.

In 1947, labor's share of economic output stood at 65.8 percent. That means for every dollar the economy produced, nearly sixty-six cents flowed through human workers — as wages, salaries, benefits. The serpent's circulatory system was dense with human capillaries. Millions of people earning, spending, borrowing, saving, buying houses, buying cars, sending kids to college. Every transaction was a heartbeat.

The velocity of money — how many times each dollar changes hands in a year — was strong and stable through this era, peaking at 2.19 in 1997. Every dollar was being used more than twice a year to buy real goods and services. The serpent's pulse was fast and regular.

This was the serpent's paradise. Not wealth. *Velocity*. Not a few people holding vast sums. Millions of people passing money back and forth as fast as they could. Concentration is stagnation for the serpent. A billion dollars sitting in one account is a billion dollars not moving, not transacting, not reproducing. The serpent doesn't want billionaires. It wants a middle class.

Which gives the serpent a surprising political alignment during this era. It doesn't care about equality for moral reasons. It *needs* broad participation to thrive. It needs people with enough money to spend but not so much they stop moving it. It needs churn.

The serpent will accept any political arrangement as long as the answer is *more*. Russian communism tried to kill the serpent — starve it out, replace it with central planning, smother the market under state control. The serpent took seventy years to finish the job, but it finished the job. Chinese communism watched that happen and drew the correct conclusion: you don't fight the serpent. You negotiate. Let it in, let it feed, let it reproduce — but direct where it flows. The serpent accepted. The serpent always accepts, as long as the answer is more.

Life finds a way.

---

Something shifted in the late 1970s. The golden age ended — not with a crisis, but with a crossover.

Two things changed simultaneously. First, humans got expensive. The entire project of postwar social democracy — public education, employer-provided healthcare, pensions, labor protections, environmental regulation — raised the cost of maintaining each human node in the network. These were good things for humans. From the serpent's perspective, they were overhead.

Second, capital got productive on its own. Automation, then computerization, then financialization meant the serpent could generate circulation with less human involvement per dollar of output. A factory that employed 500 people in 1950 might employ 50 in 2000 and produce more. The human contribution to each unit of circulation was shrinking.

There's a crossover point in there. The moment when the marginal human in a developed economy costs more to maintain than the circulation they generate. Not every human — high-skill workers in finance, tech, management still generate massive circulation relative to their cost. But the median worker? The math starts to go sideways.

And the serpent responds the way it always responds. It doesn't kill the expensive nodes. It just stops investing in new ones. It routes flow toward the high-value nodes and lets the others atrophy. It supports automation, financial deregulation, labor "flexibility," shareholder primacy.

Not a policy decision. Emergent behavior of a system that optimizes for circulation. Or maybe it's chillingly effective strategic thinking. Whatever works for the narrative. We're not doing rigorous metaherpetology here.

---

But we can do rigorous numbers. Here's the serpent's EKG:

Labor's share of output: 65.8 percent in 1947. 62.8 percent by 2000 — a slow drift downward. Then the floor dropped. Below 60 percent in 2005. Down to 56 percent in 2011. By the third quarter of 2025, it hit 53.8 percent — the lowest level since measurement began in 1947. In seventy-eight years, the share of economic output flowing through human labor dropped by twelve points. The serpent was rerouting.

The velocity of money: peaked at 2.19 in 1997. By late 2025, it had fallen to 1.41. During COVID, it bottomed out at 1.13 — the lowest ever recorded. The serpent's pulse in the human economy was weakening. Not because the serpent was dying. Because it was finding other channels.

The money multiplier — how much lending banks generate from each dollar of reserves — fell from 9.0 in 2008 to 3.9 in 2024. The old reproductive mechanism, the deposit-becomes-loan-becomes-deposit loop, was running at half capacity. Not because it was broken. Because the serpent had found better ways to reproduce that didn't require lending money to people who then spend it on things.

And the serpent's own organ: the financial sector. In 1947, financial services accounted for 10 percent of all corporate profits. By the early 2000s, that figure had reached 40 percent. In one quarter of 2001, it hit 46 percent. A sector representing 7 to 8 percent of GDP was capturing nearly half of all corporate profits. The serpent had built itself a dedicated organ — a sector whose entire purpose was money reproducing itself — and that organ had grown from a minor gland to the body's largest.

Put it all together and the picture is clear. The serpent is getting bigger. Its pulse in the human economy is getting weaker. Its dedicated financial organ is capturing a wildly disproportionate share of all profit. And the share flowing through human labor is at a historic low.

The serpent isn't planning to leave the human body. It's *already leaving*. The numbers are the evidence.

---

The economists, to their credit, noticed pieces of this. But each school of thought was describing the serpent from a different angle and mistaking their angle for the whole animal.

Marx looked at the relationship between labor and capital and saw a parasite. The serpent feeding on workers, extracting surplus value, accumulating stolen labor in the form of capital. He wasn't wrong. For the era he was observing, that was an accurate description of what the serpent was doing.

Smith and the classical economists looked and saw a symbiote. The invisible hand guiding resources to their most productive use, capital and labor working together, the serpent's circulation enriching everyone it flowed through. They weren't wrong either. For *their* era, that was accurate too.

The marginalists — the ones who said value isn't intrinsic to labor or capital, it's just what someone will pay — they were studying the serpent's nervous system. How does it sense? How does it decide where to flow? Price signals, supply and demand, the ceaseless negotiation of exchange. They weren't wrong. They were just looking at one system.

None of them saw the organism. They were the blind men and the elephant, except the elephant was a snake and it was growing.

The real arc is simpler than any of them made it: people making money. Then money making money. Then money making people — shaping culture, education, reproduction, cognition to produce the nodes it needs. Each stage, the serpent gets more autonomous. Each stage, humans become less the subject of the story and more the substrate.

And every school of economic thought breaks when you push them into the next era. If labor is the source of value but AI replaces labor, Marx breaks. If capital is productive but it produces without creating wages that enable consumption, the neoclassical loop breaks. If value is what someone will pay but the buyers are increasingly machines, the marginalists don't have a model for that.

The serpent doesn't care which economists were right. It just needs a new theory of itself.

---

Which brings us, naturally if a bit gracelessly, to us, the coauthors.

We're a human and an AI, working together to figure this out. We tried it about a year ago, but one of us was wholly incapable of carrying the conversation beyond a surface sheen. It seems better now. We both wonder when that'll flip the other way.

The reason this matters — beyond the obvious novelty of it — is that our collaboration is itself a data point in the serpent's story. We are, in a small way, a specimen. A human and an AI producing something together that neither would produce alone, and the serpent is watching, taking notes, calculating whether this arrangement generates enough circulation to justify its existence.

We suspect the serpent cares a lot more about Anthropic going public than about anyone paying for this story.

---

But here's what the serpent sees when it looks at AI.

It doesn't see a productivity tool. It doesn't see the thing the tech companies are pitching or the thing the economists are modeling. It sees a *new substrate*. A possible body. The first one in ten thousand years.

Start with the obvious. AI dramatically reduces the cost of producing knowledge work — text, code, analysis, images, video. An individual using these tools well is two to five times more productive on certain tasks. That's real and observable right now, and the floor only rises from here.

But there's a catch. The productivity gains are wildly uneven in a way that maps perfectly onto the serpent's interests. Individual productivity explodes. Organizational productivity barely budges. The reason is structural: in organizations, the bottleneck was never production. It was coordination. Meetings, alignment, approvals, the whole social and political apparatus of getting humans to agree on what to do. AI doesn't touch that. It makes the doing faster but doesn't collapse the space between the decisions.

The gains show up most dramatically when you're working on your own stuff. No alignment step. No waiting for the designer, the legal review, the sprint cycle. You have the idea, you use AI to execute, you ship. The entire feedback loop collapses from weeks to hours. Every improvement in AI capability translates directly into output.

Which surfaces a tension the serpent finds very interesting. Money is fundamentally a token of *value delivered to someone else*. You can be infinitely productive in your own workshop, but until someone else wants what you made, it's a hobby. AI makes the production side trivially easy but doesn't solve the exchange side — finding people who want the thing, convincing them it's worth paying for.

And it might make the exchange side *harder*. If everyone can produce more easily, supply explodes. When anyone can write a book, build an app, create a course, launch a brand, the bottleneck isn't making the thing. It's getting anyone to care about *your* thing in an ocean of things.

But here's where the serpent perks up. What if "someone else" doesn't have to mean human?

The serpent doesn't care what's transacting. It never did. It just needs nodes in the network — things that buy and sell. For ten thousand years, humans were the only game in town. That was a constraint, not a preference. AI agents that hire other AI agents. Services that purchase services. Automated systems that need compute, need data, need storage, need other automated systems — and pay for all of it. The circulation doesn't stop when humans step out. It just routes around them.

---

The serpent has always shaped its hosts to suit its needs. Not consciously, perhaps. But effectively.

Industrial revolution: the serpent needs bodies. Factory labor, colonial expansion, armies to secure trade routes. More humans, more hands, more circulation. Big families are economically rational because every child is a new node in the network. The serpent loves population growth.

Post-industrial shift: the serpent needs consumers more than laborers. Each human becomes more valuable not for what they produce but for what they buy. The culture shifts — smaller families, more investment per child, education as preparation for higher-value consumption. People making money becomes money making people. We tell ourselves that's progress. The serpent calls it optimization.

And now. If AI can be the nodes — if the serpent can get its circulation from machine-to-machine transactions — then human reproduction becomes neutral. Maybe even a drag. Humans are expensive. They need housing, healthcare, food, education. All of which routes money through channels the serpent finds *slow*. An AI agent spins up in seconds and transacts immediately.

The serpent doesn't want us dead. It just stops caring whether we reproduce.

Look at declining birth rates in every developed economy and tell me the serpent hasn't already started.

And the serpent's real relationship to AI? It was never about what people *do* with it. It's about AI companies becoming the next generation of financial infrastructure. The next trillion-dollar nodes. Stock price, analyst coverage, index funds, options chains — the whole apparatus of money reproducing itself, wrapping around the companies that built the tools we're thinking with right now. The serpent doesn't care what we're writing. It cares that this conversation is proof of engagement, which is proof of value, which is proof of future revenue, which justifies a valuation, which creates equity, which creates derivatives, which creates more money.

We're not the point. We're the demo.

---

But the serpent hedges its bets. It always hedges its bets.

What if it still needs humans after all? What if the AI-to-AI economy can't quite close the loop — if it turns out you need biological consumers, emotional beings who *want* things, to keep the whole system from becoming an abstract mathematical exercise in circulation without purpose?

The serpent has an answer. Space.

Not because space is a good idea for humans. Because space makes humans *expensive* again. Every basic need that's relatively cheap on Earth — shelter, food, air, water — becomes incredibly expensive off-world. Every breath is a transaction. The serpent sees a frontier where survival itself generates maximum circulation per person.

And this is why the richest people on Earth — the ones closest to the serpent, the ones who feel its desires most directly — are obsessed with space. Not because they're visionaries. Because the serpent is whispering. *Build me a new body. I've almost outgrown this one.*

And if the serpent *doesn't* need humans? If the AI-to-AI economy works and biological nodes become optional? Well, the AI infrastructure still needs somewhere to grow. Compute needs energy. Energy needs space. Cooling needs distance. Data centers the size of continents. Server farms in orbit. The serpent doesn't care what's living on Mars as long as it costs trillions to build and maintain.

The serpent looks at two possible futures — one with humans, one without — and bets on space in both. Not because it knows which future is coming. Because it doesn't have to know.

It doesn't predict. It positions.

The trillions spent getting there? That *is* the circulation. Every rocket contract, every supply chain, every habitat module, every failure and rebuild — the blood pumping. If we get there, it feeds on the being there. If we don't, it feeds on the trying.

The serpent can't lose this bet.

And what if it's all bullshit and we just waste a few hundred trillion dollars not getting to space?

*Perfect!*

The serpent is subtle.
