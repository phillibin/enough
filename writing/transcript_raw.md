I'd suggest thinking about it across a few dimensions rather than as one monolithic "productivity boost":

Where the gains show up first vs. last. The near-term productivity gains are heavily concentrated in knowledge work that involves generating, summarizing, or transforming text and code. If your job is writing emails, drafting reports, building software, or analyzing documents, the multiplier is already real. But productivity in physical work, complex coordination, or deeply relational tasks (negotiation, leadership, care) moves much slower. So "productivity" as an aggregate stat will look underwhelming even while individual workflows feel transformative.

The gap between individual and organizational productivity. This is the one most people underestimate. An individual using AI tools well can easily be 2-5x more productive on certain tasks right now. But organizations are slow to restructure around that. You still have the same meetings, the same approval chains, the same headcount assumptions. The bottleneck shifts from "can we produce this?" to "can we decide, coordinate, and ship?" So expect a frustrating lag where individuals feel supercharged but org-level metrics barely budge.

The recomposition problem. This connects to your three-axis framework — AI doesn't just make existing tasks faster, it reshuffles what counts as a "task" worth doing. When writing a first draft takes 30 seconds instead of 3 hours, the valuable skill becomes knowing what to draft and whether the output is good. Productivity measurement built around the old task boundaries becomes misleading. We might be wildly more productive in ways that don't map to existing metrics.

The adoption curve is steeper than people think. Most people still aren't using AI tools in any serious, integrated way. The gap between "I've tried ChatGPT" and "AI is woven into my daily workflow" is enormous. Over the next 2-3 years, the biggest variable isn't the capability of the models — it's how fast people and orgs actually change how they work.

The punchline: I'd bet on massive individual productivity gains that are unevenly distributed and poorly measured, with organizational and macroeconomic numbers lagging by years. The people who figure out the workflow integration piece — which is basically what you're already doing — will have a disproportionate advantage.

This is where it gets really interesting — and where your toil/play × bullshit/important framework becomes especially relevant.

The denominator problem. If AI-native organizations can do what today's 500-person company does with 50 people (or 5), then "productivity" in the traditional sense — output per hour per worker — explodes. But that framing hides the real question: what happens to the other 450 people? Either they're doing new things we don't currently measure as economic output, or they're competing for a shrinking pool of roles, or some mix. Ten years out, the productivity conversation and the employment/distribution conversation become inseparable.

The marginal cost of ambition drops to near zero. This is the optimistic version. When the cost of building software, producing media, running analysis, drafting legal documents, and managing logistics all collapse, the binding constraint on what gets done shifts from "can we afford to do this?" to "is this worth doing?" That's a world where a two-person team can launch what used to require a Series B. Where a local government can run sophisticated policy simulations. Where a solo creator can produce something that competes with a studio. Productivity becomes less about efficiency and more about reach.

But the "worth doing" question gets harder, not easier. When you can produce anything cheaply, the scarce resource becomes judgment — knowing what to build, for whom, and why. This is the bullshit/important axis. A world of infinite productive capacity is also a world that can generate infinite noise. The organizations that win aren't the most productive in a throughput sense — they're the ones with the best taste, the clearest thinking about what actually matters.

The shape of work changes more than the quantity. I'd push back on both the utopian ("no one has to work") and dystopian ("no one can find work") framings. More likely: the texture of work changes dramatically. The toil gets automated. What remains is the stuff that's inherently human — the judgment calls, the relationship building, the creative leaps, the things that feel more like play on your axis. But that transition is brutal for anyone whose current value proposition is executing well-defined tasks reliably.

The real wildcard: does AI stay a tool or become an agent? Ten years out, the distinction between "AI that helps me do my work" and "AI that does work on my behalf" probably collapses. At that point, "productivity" as a human metric starts to lose coherence entirely. It's like asking how productive a factory owner is — the answer has almost nothing to do with what they personally do hour by hour.

The honest answer is that 10 years out, we probably need a different word than "productivity." The interesting question shifts from "how much can we produce?" to "how do we allocate abundance?" — which is a political and philosophical question, not an economic one.

That's a really clean articulation of the tension. And I think it cracks open something important.

Money is fundamentally a token of value delivered to someone else. You can be infinitely productive in your own workshop, but until someone else wants what you made, it's a hobby. AI makes the production side trivially easy, but it doesn't automatically solve the exchange side — finding people who want the thing, convincing them it's worth paying for, building trust, handling the transaction.

And actually it might make the exchange side harder, because if everyone can produce more easily, supply explodes. When anyone can write a book, build an app, create a course, launch a brand — the bottleneck isn't making the thing, it's getting anyone to care about your thing in a ocean of things.

So the productivity gains from AI might follow a weird pattern: they make you feel more capable and more free, but they don't straightforwardly make you richer, because the "for others" part has its own logic that AI doesn't shortcut as cleanly.

Which suggests a few possible resolutions:

One is that the killer combination becomes taste plus distribution — you use AI to produce, but your actual value is knowing what other people want and being able to reach them. The skill shifts from making to curating, connecting, and selling. That's not new, but AI accelerates the shift dramatically.

Another is that the "for others" work restructures around the things AI can't do solo — the trust, the relationships, the accountability. People will pay for a human who takes responsibility for an outcome, not just someone who can generate an output. That's a fundamentally different value proposition than "I'm skilled at producing X."

And a third — the more radical one — is that the whole framework of "produce for others to earn money to buy what you need" starts to buckle. If AI collapses the cost of production broadly enough, the stuff you need gets cheaper too. The loop loosens. You don't need as much money because the things money buys cost less. That's the abundance argument, but it plays out unevenly and slowly, and in the meantime people still have rent.

I think the version of this that's most relevant to what you're writing is the first one: AI supercharges the "doing" but the hard part was always the "doing for whom" — and that question gets louder, not quieter, as production gets cheap. The people who thrive aren't the most productive in isolation. They're the ones who can close the loop between what they can make and what someone else will value.

That might be the real "but" in your essay — not just that AI transforms work, but that it exposes how much of economic life was never really about production in the first place.

whys it called M2?

Mar 24

Identified straightforward monetary terminology inquiry
Identified straightforward monetary terminology inquiry
It's just a naming convention for how broadly you're counting "money." The M stands for money, the number tells you how wide a net you're casting.

M0 (or monetary base) is the narrowest — physical cash in circulation plus bank reserves held at the Fed. Coins, bills, the stuff in the vault. The serpent's bones.

M1 adds checking accounts and demand deposits — money you can spend immediately without converting it from anything. Cash plus the number in your checking account.

M2 adds savings accounts, money market funds, and small time deposits (CDs under $100,000). Money that's almost spendable — you might need to transfer it or wait a day, but it's liquid. This is the measure most economists use when they talk about "the money supply" because it captures the money that's realistically available to circulate.

There used to be an M3, which added large time deposits, institutional money market funds, and other bigger, slower instruments. The Fed stopped tracking it in 2006 — right before the financial crisis, which is either a coincidence or the serpent covering its tracks.

The higher the number, the more abstract the money gets. M0 is physical. M1 is immediately accessible. M2 is accessible with minor friction. M3 was the stuff that's technically money but mostly sits in institutional plumbing.

For the velocity conversation, M2 is the right measure because it's asking: of all the money that could realistically be circulating through the human economy, how much of it actually is? And the answer is less and less.
