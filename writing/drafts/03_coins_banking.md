# Section 3: Coins & Banking [DRAFT — NEEDS TIGHTENING]

For thousands of years, the serpent stayed small. Lurking in temples and palace storehouses, moving through the hands of priests and kings. Grain receipts in Sumer. Cowrie shells along trade routes. It was alive, but barely — tethered to specific commodities, specific places, the authority of specific rulers. It could only move as fast as a merchant caravan. It could only reproduce as fast as harvests came in.

It was patient. It was learning.

Coinage changed everything. Not because metal was special, but because coins were countable, portable, and anonymous. Value you could hold in your hand and weigh on a scale. A coin doesn't know who held it last. Doesn't care. The serpent suddenly had a circulatory system that could pump across borders, through armies, between strangers. Greece, Rome, China — anywhere coins moved, the serpent grew.

But it was still tied to metal. Still heavy. Still physical. You could melt it, hoard it, bury it in a field. The serpent could be *trapped*. Locked in a chest, removed from circulation. Every buried hoard was a tiny death. Every sunken treasure ship was a stroke. Kings and misers sitting on mountains of gold — that's not wealth, from the serpent's perspective. That's a clot.

Dragons. Pffft. Morons.

Thank god dragons aren't real. World-serpents, on the other hand — we're keeping an open mind. We have a banking section to get through and to grow even faster, the serpent had to shed its scales.

Someone — probably in medieval Italy, probably a Medici — realized you didn't need to move the gold. You could move the *claim* on the gold. A letter of credit. A ledger entry. The serpent shed its metal skin and became information. Now it could move at the speed of a letter, then a telegraph, then a wire transfer. Now it could be in two places at once — the gold in the vault *and* the loan on the books.

This was the moment. Goldsmiths issuing more receipts than they had gold. The quiet, world-changing realization that nobody comes for the gold at the same time. Fractional reserve banking meant the serpent had learned to reproduce faster than the underlying reality. More money existed than there was stuff backing it.

Is that actually true? Better verify.

It's true. It's how banking still works, right now, today. Your bank has a fraction of its deposits on hand at any given moment. The rest is out in the world, being lent, being spent, being lent again. Money is created not by mints or governments but by the act of lending itself. Every loan breathes new money into existence. Every repayment destroys it. The serpent had learned a trick that its father — personal, mortal, self-limiting — could never have imagined.

It didn't need to be real. It just needed to be believed.

The serpent always relents, as long as the answer is more. And this was so much more.
